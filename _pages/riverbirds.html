Research feature

River birds

Some birds are not merely found beside rivers but their whole lives are shaped by moving water. This is a record of what we have learned about them in the Himalayas, and across the world and of the photographs that came out of it.

With Ankita Sinha, Steve Ormerod and Ramesh Krishnamurthy · Wildlife Institute of India, Senckenberg, Cardiff University

69 specialist riverine species worldwide
19 bird families they belong to
43 river reaches surveyed in the Himalaya
3,300 metres of elevation covered
2014–18 five years of repeat counts

What is a river bird?

A river and its banks form a single ecosystem. Insects emerge from the water and drift into the air; fish, nymphs and larvae live in the current; the flood itself builds the boulders, gravel bars and cut banks that line the channel. Energy crosses that boundary in both directions, all day, every day.

A small group of birds has built its entire life around that exchange. We define them by two things: they feed mostly on what the river produces — aquatic plants, aquatic and emerging insects, fish — and they occupy habitats that the river itself created, often holding linear territories strung along the channel. They nest and roost there too, not just feed. Around the world that adds up to 69 species in 19 families, from the 8–12 g torrent tyrannulet to the critically endangered 3.5 kg white-bellied heron.

Because river living has evolved independently so many times, these birds also forage in strikingly different ways: diving and swimming underwater (the dippers, the only truly aquatic songbirds), plunge-diving for fish, snatching insects from the air above the channel, or picking prey off wet rock. That variety is what makes them both fascinating and useful.

Where we work

Our field site is the Bhagirathi basin in Uttarakhand, western India — one of the headstreams of the Ganges — together with six of its first-order tributaries. The main stem runs through deep gorges for much of its length, opening in one stretch into a wide valley with braided, shingle beds. Between the highest and lowest survey reach there is a gradient of nearly 3,000 m: subtropical river at the bottom, cold torrent at the top.

That range matters. It is the reason a single basin can hold so many river specialists, and the reason a warming climate or a new dam is unlikely to affect them all in the same way.

What we have found

Four papers, one thread: river birds are a diverse and declining group, they can be counted properly even in difficult terrain, and where the river is intact they are still there.

  • Most of them are declining

    Roughly two-thirds of the world's 69 specialist riverine birds appear to be in decline, and more than 20 are near threatened or worse. For many of the most threatened, the ecology behind the decline has barely been described.

  • Five of twelve Himalayan species fell 5–10%

    Across five years of repeat counts, Brown Dipper, Plumbeous Water Redstart, White Wagtail, Common Kingfisher and White-throated Kingfisher all declined; Blue Whistling Thrush increased. Three of the declines match national trends.

  • Unmodified reaches hold the specialists

    Riverine specialists were consistently associated with the least altered stretches: faster flow, exposed bedrock, pebble and boulder banks, and intact riverside forest. Generalists were far less selective.

  • Elevation sorts the community

    River songbirds — dippers, wagtails, redstarts — increased with altitude, while kingfishers and White-browed Wagtails were concentrated below about 1,600 m. Body mass, bill and tarsus follow the same gradient, partly constrained by ancestry.

How we got here

  1. 2014–2018 Five summers on the Bhagirathi

    43 reaches, three visits each year, 32 riparian bird species recorded. Three thousand three hundred and seven individuals of the twelve species we could model in detail.

  2. 2019 The case for river birds as indicators

    Two years of survey data showed specialists tracking the least modified reaches — the first evidence from this basin that the birds could stand in for river condition. Paper →

  3. 2022 Traits, ancestry and coexistence

    Combining species traits, phylogeny and habitat over 3,000 m of elevation explained how so many similar birds divide one corridor. Paper →

  4. 2025 Estimating what the eye misses

    N-mixture models corrected raw counts for detectability and produced the first abundance trends for this community — and the first hint of decline. Paper →

  5. 2026 A global view

    The first review of all 69 species: where they are concentrated, what threatens them, and why freshwater biologists should care. Paper →

What threatens them

Nothing on this list will be unfamiliar to anyone who follows rivers. What is striking is how rarely the mechanisms have been worked out for the birds themselves — whether a dam matters because it removes riffle habitat, because it changes the drift of insects, or because it changes water temperature and with it the timing of emergence.

  • Dams and flow regulation
  • Water abstraction
  • Land-use change
  • Pollution
  • Invasive non-native species
  • Climate change
  • Loss of riparian forest
  • River-bed mining

What comes next

The two needs we argue for in the global review are the two we are working on.

  • Keep counting

    The trend signal from five years is suggestive, not conclusive. Longer runs of detectability-corrected counts — in this basin and in others — are the only way to separate real decline from variation between years.

  • Work at catchment scale

    River birds respond to what happens across a whole catchment, not just in the channel. Protecting them means protecting flow, water quality, riparian forest and connectivity together.

  • Close the ecological gaps

    For most of the 69 species, we still cannot say what limits them. Prey use, home range, breeding success and the mechanics of each threat are open questions — and good ones for freshwater biologists.
