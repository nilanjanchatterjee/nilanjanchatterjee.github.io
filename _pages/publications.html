Plain-language summaries

What this work says, without the jargon

Most of my papers are about one question: what are rivers doing for the birds that live on them, and what are those birds telling us about the rivers? Here is the short version, in everyday language, of the four papers listed below.

1. Rivers have their own birds

Some birds are not just near water — they are built for it. They feed on what the river produces, and they nest and roost in structures the river itself made: boulders, gravel bars, cut banks. Worldwide there are 69 such specialists, in 19 bird families, from a 10-gram forktail to a 3.5-kg heron. Freshwater science has largely walked past them.

2. Many are quietly disappearing

Roughly two-thirds of the 69 species appear to be declining, and more than 20 are near threatened or worse. In our own five-year Himalayan study, five of twelve species fell by about 5–10%, and for three of them national citizen-science data show the same direction. Dams, pollution, land clearing and climate are the familiar causes; how they reach the birds is mostly unknown.

3. Counting them is harder than it looks

A kingfisher that shoots past at head height is easy to miss; a dipper bobbing on a wet rock is not. Raw counts therefore compare visibility rather than abundance. We surveyed the same 43 river stretches three times a year for five years, and used a model that estimates the chance of detecting each species before working back to real numbers.

4. The birds are a signal for the whole river

Specialist species kept turning up in the least modified reaches: faster water, clean bedrock, boulder banks, intact riverside forest. Generalists were far less fussy — exactly what you want from an indicator. The practical ask is to protect rivers at the scale of whole catchments, and to put research where the species and the threats are concentrated.

Field notes, study sites and photographs from this work are collected on the River Birds page.
